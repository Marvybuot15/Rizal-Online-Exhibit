01 / THE JOURNEY

A traveler’s eye.
A writer’s memory.

In 1888, José Rizal traveled to Hong Kong and Macau after the publication of Noli Me Tángere, beginning a journey that shaped his observations of colonial society.

Hong Kong's busy port and British administration contrasted with the quieter atmosphere he recorded in Portuguese Macau. He visited Macau with José María Basa from February 18–20, 1888, then returned to Hong Kong in 1891–1892 to practice medicine and continue his reform work. Explore four places through Rizal's recorded impressions, historical context, and the ideas they invite us to consider.

Victoria City / Central District

Hong Kong offered Rizal relative freedom from Spanish censorship, but he remained alert to surveillance. He observed British administration, cleanliness, commercial liberty, and the economic influence of religious orders.

Hong Kong Protestant Cemetery

Rizal visited Protestant, Catholic, and Muslim burial grounds and admired the Protestant cemetery's greenery, orderly paths, and careful upkeep—a setting for reflecting on public dignity and respect across faiths.

MELANCHOLY · GLOOM

The City of Macau & Portuguese Quarter

Rizal described Macau as “small, low, and gloomy,” and “sad and almost dead” beside bustling Hong Kong. His diary offers a personal snapshot of the city's atmosphere in February 1888.

The Grotto of Camões

The grotto is associated with Portuguese poet Luís de Camões and the tradition of his exile in Macau. Rizal's visit invites a parallel between writers who turned hardship and displacement into lasting literary legacies.

05 / REFLECTION

Places are more
than points on a map.

They are felt, remembered, and described.

Hong Kong offered relative freedom, commercial activity, and a base for reform work; its Protestant cemetery also prompted admiration for an orderly, carefully maintained public space. Macau left Rizal with a melancholy impression and brought him to a literary landmark associated with exile. Together, these four places illuminate the relationship between place, colonial society, and a writer's sense of duty.

As you explore, consider: How can a journey influence a writer's understanding of justice, civic life, and national identity?

I

Comparison sharpens judgment

Seeing British Hong Kong beside quiet Portuguese Macau gave Rizal two colonial systems to weigh against his own experience of Spanish rule.

II

Civic space shows values

His admiration for the orderly Protestant cemetery points to a recurring interest in public dignity, hygiene, and respect across faiths.

III

A diary is one viewpoint

Rizal's "gloomy" Macau is a snapshot from February 1888, not a complete portrait of the city. Read first impressions critically.

IV

Writers carry exile forward

At the Grotto of Camões, displacement and literary legacy meet, a parallel that echoes Rizal's own life as a writer.
